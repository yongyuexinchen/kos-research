## Title

**Is "desire quenching" — the preemptive killing of one's own desires before reality can disappoint — a recognizable mechanism, and is it becoming the default strategy under late capitalism?**

---

## Body

I've been sitting on an observation for a while, and I want to bring it to this community because I think it touches on something that critical theory has gestured at but not quite named — and because I'm aware that I might be re-describing something already theorized better.

**The observation:** There's a pattern I keep noticing, in myself and others, where someone wants something intensely for years — a relationship, a career, a creative project — and then, precisely when it comes within reach, the desire collapses. Not because the thing turned out to be bad. Not from burnout. But as if the wanting itself had been the real object, and the attainment is somehow a *threat* to it.

**My attempt at a mechanism (five parts):**
1. **Imagination outruns reality.** Long anticipation builds an imagined version of the object that no actual version can match.
2. **The reference point is pushed to maximum.** Anticipation raises the internal benchmark to the ideal — so reality at 90 is experienced as a loss of 10, even when it's genuinely good. (This is close to Kahneman & Tversky's reference-dependence, though they're not usually read this way.)
3. **Fantasy pre-consumes the experience.** The imagined enjoyment is partially spent during the years of waiting, so the real event delivers diminishing returns.
4. **Completion creates a vacuum.** When the goal has been the horizon for years, its achievement removes the horizon itself — completion is experienced as loss of structure.
5. **The defensive withdrawal.** And so the subject withdraws *before* steps 2-4 can occur, protecting the narrative "I could have had it" against the worse outcome "I had it and it disappointed."

**Why I think this connects to Critical Theory:** This seems to me like the subjective correlate of what Marcuse called repressive desublimation — desire is not suppressed but managed, redirected, and in this case *preemptively liquidated*. It also resonates with what Fisher (in *Capitalist Realism*) described as the erosion of the capacity to imagine alternatives: if you can't imagine a future in which the desire would be satisfied anyway, killing the desire becomes the rational move. The question that interests me is whether this is a **timeless human trait** (the ancient trope of the fox and the grapes) or whether late capitalism has *industrialized* it — turning preemptive withdrawal into the default orientation toward life, visible in everything from the "soulmate fantasy" that makes real relationships feel like downgrades, to the dream job that feels empty once the offer arrives, to declining desire for the future itself.

**Where I want to be challenged:**
- Is this just "defensive pessimism" (Norem & Cantor) or "fear of failure" with a new name? What's genuinely distinct about *desire* itself collapsing rather than expectations being lowered?
- Is the withdrawal actually preemptive, or is it rational updating — the subject correctly recognizing that the object was overvalued? How would we even distinguish these empirically?
- Is my claim that this is *more common now* defensible, or is it survivor bias / projection from my own experience?
- And the sharpest version: does theorizing "desire quenching" risk becoming an apology for the present — describing the liquidation of desire as a psychological invariant when it may be a historically specific, political condition that could be otherwise?

I'll be honest that the origin of this idea is personal (I observed it in myself around something I'd wanted for years), and I'm trying to figure out whether it's a genuine mechanism worth theorizing or a private experience I've over-generalized. The community's assessment — especially where this has already been said, and better — would be genuinely useful.
