# CMV Post 1: EGS Theory (r/changemyview)

> **Post as: 永月 (Yong Yue) — 英文标题 + 正文**

---

## Title

**CMV: Society's promises die before its punishments — and this asymmetry, not "laziness," is why so many people today have given up on effort, marriage, and children.**

---

## Body

I want to challenge a view I've been developing, and I genuinely want to hear where it breaks.

**The view:** Every society runs on an "existential guarantee system" (EGS) — a set of positive promises ("work hard → good life") paired with negative punishments ("if you don't comply, you lose"). My claim is that when a society's guarantees start failing, these two components decay at different speeds:

- **Positive promises die fast.** People stop believing "effort → reward." The promise "study hard → good job → good life" stops being credible.
- **Negative punishments die slow.** People still fear the consequences of non-compliance — losing status, income, social standing — even after they've stopped believing in the rewards.

**The result:** A society where fear outlives hope. People keep running — not because they believe the race leads anywhere, but because stopping feels dangerous. This produces the paradoxes we see everywhere: record-low fertility in societies that claim to want children, people "quiet quitting" while still fearing unemployment, young people avoiding marriage not because they don't want love but because the institution's promises no longer hold.

**Why I believe this:**
1. The asymmetry is observable: you can measure "do you believe effort pays off?" vs "do you fear what happens if you stop?" — and the gap between them seems to grow as institutional trust falls.
2. It explains data points that individual-level psychology struggles with: fertility decline in wealthy societies (where the *costs* of raising children rose faster than the *promises* of family life delivered), the rise of "escapist" consumption (anesthesia rather than hope).
3. It predicts something testable: people with low "expected fulfillment" should show a specific pattern — fear of non-compliance remains high while belief in rewards collapses.

**Where I want to be challenged:**
- Is this just "institutional trust" with extra steps? What does this theory explain that existing frameworks (anomie, alienation, trust theory) don't?
- Is the asymmetry actually real, or do promises and punishments decay together? I'd love evidence of societies where punishments collapsed faster than promises.
- Am I over-generalizing from modern wealthy societies to all of history?

I'm genuinely open to being wrong — if you can show the asymmetry doesn't hold, or that existing theory explains the same data with fewer assumptions, I'll award a delta.
