# CMV Post 2: DCM / Desire Quenching (r/changemyview)

> **Post as: 永月 (Yong Yue) — 英文标题 + 正文**

---

## Title

**CMV: People don't lose desire because they're "depressed" or "lazy" — they preemptively kill their own desires before reality can disappoint them, and modern life has made this the default strategy.**

---

## Body

I want to challenge a view I've been developing, and I want to hear where it breaks.

**The view:** There's a psychological mechanism I call "desire quenching" (欲望熄火). When someone has wanted something for a long time — a relationship, a career, a dream — and that thing finally comes within reach, they often experience a strange collapse of desire. Not because the thing is bad. Not because they're lazy. But because **the imagined version has become more real to them than any actual version could be.**

**The mechanism (five parts):**
1. **Imagination > reality.** Years of wanting something builds a perfect imagined version. Any real version loses to it.
2. **The reference point gets pushed to 100.** Anticipation raises your internal benchmark to the ideal. Reality at 90 feels like a loss of 10 — so the real thing feels worse than the imagined thing even when it's genuinely good.
3. **Fantasy pre-consumes the experience.** The imagined enjoyment is partially "spent" during the waiting years. The real event delivers diminishing returns.
4. **Completion creates a vacuum.** After years of "the goal" being your horizon, achieving it leaves nothing ahead. Completion itself feels like a loss.
5. **The defensive withdrawal (my addition).** So people withdraw *before* step 2-4 can happen. "I'm not unable to have it — I choose not to want it." This protects the self-narrative: "I could have had it" beats "I had it and it disappointed."

**Why I believe this:**
- It explains the pattern where people pursue something for years, then abandon it right at the finish line — not out of burnout, but *as a defense against anticipated disappointment*.
- It connects to documented effects: reference-point theory (Kahneman & Tversky), defensive pessimism, the Zeigarnik effect.
- It predicts modern phenomena: the "soulmate" fantasy that makes real relationships feel like downgrades; the dream job that feels empty after the offer arrives.

**Where I want to be challenged:**
- Is this just "defensive pessimism" or "fear of failure" with a new name? What's genuinely different about "desire quenching"?
- Is the withdrawal really *preemptive*, or is it just rational updating ("I now realize this isn't worth it")? How would you distinguish the two?
- Is this actually more common now, or is it a timeless human trait I'm projecting onto modern life?

I'm genuinely open to being wrong. If you can show this reduces to existing mechanisms, or that the "preemptive" claim doesn't survive scrutiny, I'll award a delta.
